When somebody finds out that I live in Costa Rica, the questions normally start very quickly.

How expensive is it?

Can I get an IT job there?

Can I manage without Spanish?

Where should I live?

Is Costa Rica safe?

How does healthcare work?

Can my spouse come with me?

What about Indian food?

Do I need a car?

And eventually:

**Would you actually recommend moving there?**

I have been living in Costa Rica for years now, and I have answered some version of these questions many times—especially for people coming from India for technology jobs and, increasingly, people from the United States thinking about remote work or simply a different way of living.

I even made a YouTube FAQ about many of these things years ago.

But living somewhere for a long time changes the way you answer the question.

When you first arrive, you notice the volcanoes, beaches, green mountains and weather.

After some years, you start noticing different things.

How long does a government process take?

Which supermarket is expensive?

Which route should you absolutely avoid at 5:00 PM?

Why is something on Amazon $40 but suddenly feels like a luxury item here?

Which neighborhood makes your daily life easier?

And why does a twenty-minute distance on Google Maps sometimes take an hour?

So this is not really a tourist guide.

It is the conversation I would have with someone who told me:

> “Sakir, I am seriously thinking about moving to Costa Rica. Tell me what I should know before I do it.”

## First: I genuinely like living here

Let me start there because otherwise the rest of this article may sound like a long list of warnings.

Costa Rica has been very good to me.

The country is beautiful, of course. You can drive a few hours and completely change your surroundings—mountains, volcanoes, rainforest, Pacific beaches, Caribbean beaches, hot springs.

But that is not actually what makes somewhere home.

For me, one of Costa Rica's biggest advantages is the balance.

You can have a serious technology career and still live somewhere where nature is constantly around you. You can work during the week and spend a weekend in La Fortuna, Guanacaste, Manuel Antonio or somewhere in the mountains without taking a flight.

Costa Rica also operates in a time zone that works extremely well with North American companies. That matters much more than people realize if you work in technology.

The country's service and technology economy is substantial rather than something built only around tourism. In the first half of 2026, Costa Rica exported more than $6 billion in services excluding travel, and ICT services grew 7% year over year. <a href="https://www.comex.go.cr/sala-de-prensa/comunicados/2026/octubre/cp-3267-exportaciones-de-servicios-superan-los-6000-millones-y-crecen-3-al-primer-semestre-servicios-empresariales-lideran-el-dinamismo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Comex</a>

That is part of the reason so many multinational technology, shared-services, engineering and business operations exist here.

But Costa Rica is not perfect.

And it is definitely not cheap paradise.

That is probably the first misconception I would remove.

## How much does it really cost to live in Costa Rica?

This is the question I get most.

And my answer now is:

**Tell me where you want to live and how you want to live first.**

There is no useful single “Costa Rica cost of living.”

Someone sharing an apartment in Heredia, cooking at home and taking buses can have a completely different monthly expense from someone renting a furnished condominium in Santa Ana, owning a car, eating out and traveling every weekend.

### Housing will decide most of your budget

Look at current Santa Ana listings and you will quickly see two-bedroom apartments around roughly $1,150–$1,600, with newer, furnished or more premium properties going higher. Current San José listings similarly show modern one- and two-bedroom units easily reaching $1,250–$1,800 depending on location and furnishing. <a href="https://www.encuentra24.com/costa-rica-es/bienes-raices-alquiler-apartamentos/san-jose-provincia-santa-ana?q=f_rooms.2-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">encuentra24.com</a>

That does **not** mean you cannot find cheaper housing.

You absolutely can.

But it demonstrates why I don't like telling someone:

> “You need exactly $X per month to live in Costa Rica.”

One decision about housing can change your monthly expenses by $700 or $1,000.

### My planning number

If a single professional asked me today what they should budget before arriving, I would personally be much more comfortable planning around **$1,500–$2,000+ per month** if they want to live independently in the Greater Metropolitan Area.

You may spend less.

You can also very easily spend more.

For a couple or family, I would start the planning conversation closer to **$2,500+**, and then look separately at housing, transportation, healthcare, childcare or school, and lifestyle.

Those are planning buffers, not official Costa Rican averages.

That distinction matters.

I would rather someone arrive with extra room in their budget than build their entire relocation around the cheapest apartment they managed to find online.

## Where should you live?

Another question where people want one answer.

There isn't one.

For most professionals, I would first look at **where you will work**.

Then choose where you live.

Not the other way around.

### Santa Ana and the west side

Santa Ana, Lindora and nearby areas are popular with professionals and expatriates.

There are modern condominiums, restaurants, shopping, gyms and office parks.

The trade-off is simple:

**it is not the cheap version of Costa Rica.**

If your office is nearby or you work remotely and like the area, it can be a very comfortable place to live.

### Heredia

Heredia makes a lot of sense if you work around the large business parks and technology operations there.

It also has good access to the airport depending on the exact area.

Again, check commute time rather than distance.

### San José

San José itself gives you more urban life, restaurants, hospitals, offices and services.

Neighborhood matters enormously.

There is a huge difference between different parts of San José, so I would never rent something for a year based only on an attractive online listing.

### Alajuela

Alajuela and surrounding residential areas can give you better value, particularly if you do not need to be in central San José every morning.

Airport access is another advantage.

### Beach towns

Living near Tamarindo, Jacó, Nosara or another coastal area sounds amazing.

And it can be.

But vacation Costa Rica and Tuesday-morning-working-from-home Costa Rica are different things.

Before committing to a beach town, check:

- internet at the exact property
- electricity reliability
- access to healthcare
- supermarkets
- transportation
- heat and humidity
- rainy-season conditions
- how often you actually need to go to San José

I would stay somewhere for a few weeks before signing a long lease.

That one decision can save you a lot of frustration.

## Food: cook at home and discover the feria

One thing I still recommend exactly as strongly as I did years ago:

**find your local feria.**

A *feria del agricultor* is the local farmers' market.

For fruits, vegetables and many fresh products, it can be one of the nicest and most economical parts of living here.

It also becomes part of your routine.

You go on Saturday or Sunday morning, buy what you need for the week, maybe get some fruit you have never tried before, and slowly your grocery shopping stops feeling like something you are doing in a foreign country.

Supermarkets are convenient, but imported products can become expensive very quickly.

Current San José consumer-price data, for example, puts a basic inexpensive restaurant meal around ₡5,000 and a mid-range dinner for two around ₡30,000, although prices obviously vary widely. <a href="https://www.numbeo.com/cost-of-living/in/San-Jose-Costa-Rica" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Numbeo</a>

### For Indians: yes, you can find Indian food

You can find Indian restaurants and Indian groceries.

But if you expect Indian food to cost what it costs in Mumbai, Delhi or Hyderabad, you will be disappointed.

Eating Indian food outside regularly can get expensive.

Imported groceries can also carry a big premium.

My approach has always been simple:

**cook most normal meals at home and treat eating out as eating out.**

Rice, vegetables, chicken, eggs, beans, fruit and local staples are straightforward.

Special imported brands are where your grocery bill starts behaving differently.

## Should you bring Indian groceries with you?

Yes—but don't turn your suitcase into a supermarket.

Bring difficult-to-find personal items and spices you genuinely use.

But there is an important customs point here.

Costa Rica has strict phytosanitary controls. The country's State Phytosanitary Service specifically warns that seeds, grains, plants, fruits, vegetables, teas, spices and other plant material may be restricted and that requirements vary by product, preparation and country of origin. Items without the appropriate requirements can be retained or destroyed. <a href="https://www.sfe.go.cr/SitePages/Tramites/ComprasenLinea.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SFE</a>

So my practical rule is:

**do not assume that something is allowed simply because it is commercially packaged.**

Check before you fly.

For me, that is much easier than having an argument with agriculture officials at the airport over a packet of food.

## Internet and working remotely

This part of Costa Rica has improved significantly.

If you are living in the Greater Metropolitan Area or another well-connected area, working remotely is completely realistic.

Fiber availability is increasingly common, but it is still something I would verify **at the exact property** before signing a lease.

Don't ask:

> “Does Santa Ana have fiber?”

Ask:

> “Can this specific apartment get fiber from this provider?”

There is a difference.

As one current reference point, Kölbi advertises home internet plans including 100 Mbps at ₡15,000 per month before its modem rental, subject to technical availability. <a href="https://www.kolbi.cr/wps/portal/kolbi/hogares/servicios-kolbi-hogar/internet/otros%20planes/internet-hogar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Kölbi</a>

Other providers compete at different speeds and price points.

For somebody working as a software engineer, I would also have mobile data as backup.

Video calls, VPNs, cloud development environments and remote desktops are not places where I want to discover that my apartment has bad connectivity after moving in.

## What about IT jobs?

This is one area where Costa Rica has always made sense to me professionally.

There is a mature multinational-services ecosystem here.

Software development, cloud, cybersecurity, data, automation, AI, support, finance, engineering and shared-service roles all exist.

In 2026 alone there have continued to be new technology expansions, including software, AI/ML and cybersecurity positions. <a href="https://www.comex.go.cr/sala-de-prensa/comunicados/2026/mayo/cp-3223-empresa-de-tecnolog%C3%ADa-feuji-se-expande-en-costa-rica-y-abrir%C3%A1-hasta-150-nuevos-puestos-de-trabajo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Comex</a>

Historically, people have asked me:

> “What is the average software engineer salary?”

I don't like giving one number anymore.

The range is too wide.

A local company, multinational, consulting company, nearshore provider and U.S. company hiring an international contractor can all pay very differently for somebody with the same number of years of experience.

Your English also matters.

Your specialization matters.

And seniority matters.

For software engineers, I would focus particularly on:

- .NET / Java / backend engineering
- React / Angular / modern frontend
- Azure / AWS / cloud
- DevOps
- data engineering
- cybersecurity
- AI and applied machine learning
- automation and QA engineering

More important than chasing an “average salary” is understanding **net compensation**.

Costa Rica has progressive salary income-tax brackets. For 2026, employment income moves through brackets from tax-exempt income into 10%, 15%, 20% and 25% marginal rates; those percentages apply progressively rather than simply taxing your entire salary at the highest bracket. <a href="https://www.hacienda.go.cr/docs/TramosRenta2026.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ministerio de Hacienda</a>

Social-security deductions and benefits also matter.

So compare offers on what actually reaches you and what benefits you receive—not only the gross USD number somebody posts online.

## Remote U.S. job versus Costa Rican employment

This deserves its own section because these are two completely different situations.

If a Costa Rican company or multinational employs you locally, your immigration, payroll, social-security and tax situation follows that arrangement.

If you are working remotely for a foreign company, that does **not automatically mean you have permission to live or work locally indefinitely**.

Costa Rica has a specific digital-nomad framework for qualifying remote workers whose income comes from outside the country.

The detailed official requirements currently specify at least **$3,000 per month in foreign-source income for an individual, or $4,000 when applying with dependents**, along with documentation and medical insurance requirements. <a href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/digital-nomads/requirements" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Visit Costa Rica</a>

This is exactly why I separate three questions:

**Where is my employer?**

**Where am I legally allowed to live?**

**Where am I legally allowed to work?**

They are related.

They are not the same question.

## Visas and residency: don't plan this from WhatsApp advice

Immigration is the section where I would be the most careful about taking advice from friends—including me.

Rules change.

Nationality matters.

Your reason for staying matters.

Your family situation matters.

Costa Rica's current entry guidance requires a valid passport, a visa where applicable, proof of economic means and return or onward travel. The immigration officer determines the authorized duration of stay. <a href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/planning-your-trip/entry-requirements" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Visit Costa Rica</a>

If you are coming from the United States with a U.S. passport, the tourist-entry situation is generally much simpler.

For an Indian passport holder, I would check the current rules directly with Costa Rica's consular office in New Delhi before booking anything significant. Costa Rica maintains an official consular office there specifically for these matters. <a href="https://www.rree.go.cr/?cat=deCR&amp;cont=525&amp;id=176&amp;pais=IN&amp;sec=misiones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Worship</a>

Do not build a relocation around something somebody told you happened to them two years ago.

Check your **exact passport + exact status + exact travel date**.

### DIMEX is an ID, not the immigration category itself

You will hear the word **DIMEX** constantly.

Think of DIMEX as the identification document issued to many foreigners who have been granted a legal immigration status.

The underlying status might be permanent residency, temporary residency, a special category or another authorized stay. <a href="https://www.migracion.go.cr/Documentos%20compartidos/Normativa%20Legal%20Migratoria/Circular%20DIMEX%20que%20se%20encuentran%20actualmente%20vigentes.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Migración Costa Rica</a>

That distinction becomes important when discussing employment.

Costa Rican immigration law has specific categories and authorizations covering different kinds of foreign workers. <a href="https://www.migracion.go.cr/Documentos%20compartidos/Leyes/Ley%20General%20de%20Migraci%C3%B3n%20y%20Extranjer%C3%ADa%208764.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Migración Costa Rica</a>

If a company is relocating you, involve their immigration team early.

If you are handling residency independently, use Migración or a qualified immigration professional rather than designing your plan around Facebook comments.

## Moving with your spouse or family

This is one of those topics that looks simple until you start collecting documents.

Marriage certificates.

Birth certificates.

Apostilles.

Translations.

Police records.

Insurance.

Immigration appointments.

My advice is to start collecting documents **before** you move.

Not after.

Also remember that the immigration status of the primary applicant and the rights of a dependent are not necessarily identical.

If both spouses intend to work, verify what each person's status allows.

If you have children, I would decide the school question before the house question.

Private and bilingual schools can become one of the largest family expenses, and driving across the Greater Metropolitan Area twice every school day can completely change your experience of living here.

## Healthcare

Costa Rica has two realities operating side by side.

The public system is run through the **Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social—CCSS**, normally just called *la Caja*.

People legally enrolled in the system generally begin through their assigned local EBAIS for primary care and can be referred into clinics and hospitals when necessary. CCSS confirms that foreigners legally residing in the country can participate in the social-security system under the applicable rules. <a href="https://aissfa.ccss.sa.cr/contacto2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CCSS</a>

Then there is private healthcare.

Private doctors, laboratories, specialists and hospitals are widely used, especially when someone wants faster scheduling or a specific specialist.

Many people effectively use both systems depending on the situation.

If you are moving from the United States, private healthcare prices may feel surprisingly reasonable in comparison.

If you are moving from India, that comparison may feel very different.

That is why “healthcare is cheap in Costa Rica” is too simplistic.

The better question is:

**What system will I be covered by, and what will I use for normal care versus urgent or specialist care?**

## Banking will probably annoy you at least once

Costa Rica's banking system works.

But compliance is serious.

Expect to explain:

- who you are
- where your income comes from
- your immigration status
- your employer
- sometimes the source of funds

This is normal KYC/anti-money-laundering procedure.

For example, Banco Nacional's current requirements distinguish between residents and non-residents: resident foreigners can use DIMEX or another recognized immigration ID, while non-residents can open certain accounts in person using a valid passport and proof of lawful stay. <a href="https://www.bncr.fi.cr/hubfs/T%C3%A9rminos%20y%20condiciones%20Cuenta%20Expediente%20Simplificado.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">BNCR</a>

Once your banking is established, daily life becomes much easier.

But I would arrive with an international card and financial backup rather than expecting to land Monday and have every Costa Rican banking service working Tuesday.

For sending money internationally, compare the full cost—not only the advertised transfer fee.

Exchange-rate spread can sometimes matter more than the visible fee.

## Do you need a car?

You can live without one.

Whether you will **want** to live without one is a different question.

Costa Rica has an extensive bus network and buses can be very economical.

Ride-hailing also makes life easier in urban areas.

If you live close to work, work remotely and mainly stay within the Central Valley, you can absolutely delay buying a car.

But once you start exploring Costa Rica seriously, a car changes the experience.

The problem is that cars introduce another set of expenses:

insurance, maintenance, fuel, registration, parking and repairs.

And then there is traffic.

This deserves emphasis:

**Do not judge Costa Rican travel by kilometers.**

A destination 20 km away may not feel like a 20 km journey.

Traffic around San José, Heredia, Alajuela and Santa Ana can completely change commute times.

Check the route during the actual hour you will travel.

Not Sunday afternoon.

### Driving as a visitor

Costa Rica's tourism authority currently states that visitors can use a valid foreign driver's licence for up to three months without needing an international driving permit. <a href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Visit Costa Rica</a>

If you're becoming a resident, check the rules applicable to your immigration status rather than assuming tourist rules continue forever.

## Is Costa Rica safe?

My answer is neither:

> “Costa Rica is completely safe.”

nor:

> “Costa Rica is dangerous.”

Both are useless.

You live normally here.

But you use common sense.

Do not leave laptops or bags visible inside your car.

Be careful with phones and valuables.

Think twice about empty streets or isolated beaches late at night.

Understand the specific neighborhood where you are renting.

The current U.S. State Department advisory places Costa Rica at **Level 2: Exercise Increased Caution**, specifically because of crime, noting both petty crime and more serious incidents. <a href="https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/costa-rica.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Travel.state.gov</a>

That does not mean you spend your life afraid.

I don't.

It means you do the same thing you should do in most countries:

learn where you are and behave accordingly.

## How much Spanish do you actually need?

You can survive with English.

I would not recommend building your life around surviving.

Inside multinational companies and technology teams, you may work almost completely in English.

Then you leave the office.

Your mechanic speaks Spanish.

The delivery driver calls you in Spanish.

A government office speaks Spanish.

Someone asks you something at the feria.

You need to explain something to a doctor, security guard or taxi driver.

That is when even basic Spanish dramatically improves your life.

You don't need to arrive fluent.

But learn.

I wish more people treated Spanish as part of moving to Costa Rica rather than an optional hobby after moving.

## The weather is not simply “tropical”

One thing people underestimate is how much Costa Rica changes over a relatively small distance.

The Central Valley can have beautiful mild weather.

Drive toward Guanacaste and it can feel much hotter and drier.

Go toward the Caribbean and the rainfall pattern changes again.

Altitude makes a huge difference.

Official tourism guidance broadly describes the dry period as around December through April and the green/rainy period around May through November, but also emphasizes that regional microclimates differ substantially. <a href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/plan-your-trip/when-to-visit" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Visit Costa Rica</a>

The rainy season also does not necessarily mean twenty-four hours of rain.

There are plenty of days where the morning is beautiful and rain arrives later.

After you live here, you simply start planning around it.

And you learn not to leave home without considering whether you will need an umbrella six hours later.

## If you're Indian, you are not coming to a place with no Indian community

I think this matters psychologically when someone moves far away from home.

Costa Rica has an established Indian community.

The **Costa Rica India Association (CRIA)** has been around since 2010 and organizes cultural activities including Diwali, Holi and other community events, as well as providing support to newcomers. <a href="https://criaindian.org/events/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CRIA</a>

You will find Indian families, technology professionals, businesses and restaurants here.

Is it the same as living somewhere with hundreds of thousands of Indians?

Obviously not.

But you are not alone.

And after a while your circle usually becomes a mix of Indian, Costa Rican and international friends anyway.

That is one of the parts of living abroad that I actually value.

## What if you are bringing a dog or cat?

Plan it properly rather than treating the pet as extra luggage.

Costa Rica's SENASA currently requires dogs and cats entering the country to meet veterinary-health requirements, including a clinical examination and official health certificate; vaccination and parasite-treatment requirements also apply depending on the animal. <a href="https://www.senasa.go.cr/informacion/estado-sanitario/alertas-sanitarias/63-cat-informacion/cat-noticias-y-eventos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SENASA Costa Rica</a>

Airlines have their own rules on top of this.

Start early.

With pets, the return journey can sometimes require as much planning as the arrival because your destination country will have its own import requirements.

## The things that are harder than people expect

Now the part friends normally hear from me after I have said all the nice things.

### Bureaucracy

Sometimes something that sounds like it should require one document requires six.

And one of those documents needs an apostille.

And another needs a translation.

And then somebody tells you that you need an appointment.

You learn patience.

### Addresses

Costa Rican addresses can be an experience.

Instead of the neat street-number logic somebody from the U.S. may expect, you may encounter directions that effectively describe your location relative to a landmark.

Eventually it starts making sense.

Mostly.

### Imported products

Anything imported can surprise you.

Electronics.

Specialty food.

Certain clothes.

Car parts.

Things that feel normal in the United States or India can be much more expensive here.

### Traffic

Costa Rica is not a large country.

That does not mean getting across it is fast.

Mountains, narrow roads, accidents, construction and traffic make distance deceptive.

### Everything takes a little longer

This was probably one of my bigger adjustments.

I come from a technology background.

My brain likes processes.

Input.

Output.

Done.

Real life is not always an API.

Costa Rica taught me that very effectively.

## What became easier than I expected

There is another side.

Once you are established, Costa Rica becomes comfortable surprisingly quickly.

You build your normal supermarket route.

You know your bank.

You know which road to avoid.

You know your doctor.

You understand enough Spanish to solve daily problems.

You find your favorite restaurants.

You stop converting every colón into dollars or rupees.

And eventually you stop thinking:

> “I am living in Costa Rica.”

You simply think:

> “I am going home.”

That transition is difficult to explain until it happens.

## What I wish somebody had told me before I moved

If I could talk to myself before moving here, I would probably say these things.

**Don't optimize your first year for the cheapest possible lifestyle.**

Optimize it for making the transition easy.

Live reasonably close to work.

Pay a little more for a safe, convenient area if you can afford it.

Get reliable internet.

Don't immediately buy everything.

Spend some time understanding the country.

Learn basic Spanish from day one.

Keep copies of every important document.

Bring apostilled documents you may need later.

Do not assume immigration rules because they worked for somebody else.

Do not underestimate traffic.

And don't spend every weekend inside San José.

That last one is important.

One reason to live in Costa Rica is Costa Rica.

Go see it.

## Mistakes I see newcomers make

The first is choosing a house before understanding their commute.

The second is comparing every price to India.

The third is comparing every service to the United States.

The fourth is refusing to learn Spanish because “everyone at my office speaks English.”

And the fifth is expecting Costa Rica to behave like the country they left.

That last one is probably universal for immigration.

If you move somewhere new but demand that everything work exactly like home, you will spend a lot of energy being irritated.

There are things Costa Rica does better.

There are things India does better.

There are things the United States does better.

You slowly build your own combination of what matters.

## Is Costa Rica right for you?

I think Costa Rica can be a very good fit if you value:

- nature
- a slower pace outside work
- relatively easy access to the United States
- remote work or the multinational-services ecosystem
- year-round warm weather
- outdoor travel
- a multicultural environment
- living somewhere that can still feel peaceful without being disconnected from modern professional life

I would think more carefully if your priorities are:

- extremely low living costs
- huge-city nightlife
- cheap imported products
- highly efficient bureaucracy
- living entirely in English
- perfect public transportation everywhere
- getting anywhere quickly just because the distance is short

Costa Rica is not India with beaches.

It is not California at half price.

And it is definitely not the permanent vacation that Instagram sometimes makes it look like.

It is a real country where you eventually have normal problems.

Bills.

Traffic.

Appointments.

Work.

Groceries.

Rain.

Internet.

Family.

And that is actually the point.

## Would I move to Costa Rica again?

Yes.

Knowing everything I know now, I would.

But I would arrive differently.

I would worry less about finding the absolute cheapest apartment.

I would learn Spanish faster.

I would organize my documents better.

I would spend more time understanding neighborhoods before choosing one.

And I would give myself a bigger financial buffer during the first few months.

Costa Rica has become much more than a country where I happened to work.

It became part of my life.

And when somebody from India or the United States asks whether they should move here, I don't try to convince them.

I tell them the good parts.

I tell them the frustrating parts.

And then I tell them to come for a few weeks if they can.

Work from here.

Buy groceries.

Sit in traffic.

Go to the feria.

Visit a doctor.

Take a bus.

Drive outside San José.

Spend a rainy afternoon at home.

Talk to people.

Because you don't really learn whether you can **live** somewhere by spending four days at a resort.

And if after experiencing the ordinary version of Costa Rica you still find yourself thinking,

**“I could see myself living here,”**

then you probably have your answer.

## A quick note before you move

Immigration, tax and entry rules change, and your situation will depend on nationality, employment and immigration category. This article reflects my experience plus information researched in October 2026; for an actual move, I would verify the current requirements directly with the relevant Costa Rican authority.
