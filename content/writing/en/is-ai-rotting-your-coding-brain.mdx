There is a small moment many developers now recognize.

You open an empty file to write something simple. A recursive function. A regex. A retry loop with backoff.

And your hands wait for the autocomplete.

The internet has a name for this feeling: **AI brain rot**.

It is a catchy phrase. It is also mostly wrong.

After reading the research, I don't think AI is damaging anyone's brain. But it can quietly stop us from learning, and the skill it hurts most is the one we need to supervise it:

**Debugging.**

The good news is that the same research points to a fix. It is not "use AI less."

It is **use AI differently**.

In this post I'll look at what the research actually says, what AI-first teams are doing in practice, which skills matter now, and the habits I'd use to stay sharp and current.

---

## What the research actually says

Before writing this, I wanted to know whether "AI brain rot" was a real thing or just a good headline.

Four findings hold up. They all point in the same direction.

### 1. AI can stop you from learning something new

In January 2026, Anthropic published a [randomized controlled trial](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) with 52 mostly junior Python developers. Each one had to learn Trio, an async library none of them had used before.

Half could use an AI assistant that would write the full solution on request. Half coded by hand.

Then everyone took a quiz on what they had just used.

- AI group: **50%**
- Hand-coding group: **67%**

That is almost two letter grades. And the AI group finished only about two minutes faster, a difference that was not statistically significant.

The biggest gap was on debugging questions.

### 2. How you use AI matters more than whether you use it

This is the part I found most useful.

The researchers looked at how each person in the AI group actually worked and found six patterns. Three of them handed the thinking to AI. Three kept the developer thinking.

| How they used AI | Quiz score | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generated code, then asked follow-up questions | 86% | 24 min |
| Asked for code plus explanations | 68% | 24 min |
| Asked only conceptual questions | 65% | 22 min |
| *Hand-coding group average (no AI)* | *67%* | *~25 min* |
| Delegated everything to AI | 39% | 19.5 min |
| Started asking, then gradually delegated | 35% | 22 min |
| Relied on AI to debug and verify their code | 24% | 31 min |

Each pattern had only 2 to 7 developers, so I read this as a direction, not a precise ranking.

But the direction is clear. The developers who only asked conceptual questions hit plenty of errors, fixed them on their own, and were still the second-fastest group overall.

**Learning and speed are not always a trade-off.**

### 3. It is not only a coding problem

A [field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), published in PNAS in 2025, found the same shape.

With GPT-4 available during practice, grades went up 48%.

When it was taken away for the exam, those students did **17% worse** than students who never had it.

A tutor version with learning guardrails mostly removed that harm. The tool was not the problem. The way it was used was.

### 4. You cannot feel it happening

This one surprised me most.

In [METR's 2025 study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), 16 experienced open-source developers worked on real issues in projects they knew well.

- Before: they expected AI to make them **24% faster**.
- After: they believed it had made them **20% faster**.
- Measured: it made them **19% slower**.

METR now says that number is out of date, because the tools have improved. Their 2026 follow-up stalled partly because many developers refused to work without AI at all.

But the lesson that stays is the perception gap:

**Feeling productive and being productive are different measurements.**

### Supporting signals

- [Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon](https://www.theregister.com/2025/02/11/microsoft_study_ai_critical_thinking/) surveyed 319 knowledge workers. The more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they reported. The more they trusted their own skills, the more they thought critically.
- In [Stack Overflow's 2025 survey](https://tech.co/news/survey-developers-using-ai-more-trust-less), 84% of developers used or planned to use AI tools, but only 33% trusted their accuracy. The most common complaint was code that is almost right, but not quite.

That last point matters.

"Almost right" code is exactly what you need strong debugging and code-reading skills to catch.

## What the research does not say

I think it matters to be honest here, because the scary version of this story spreads faster than the accurate one.

**Is AI rotting your brain? Not proven. Can it put your learning at risk? Yes.**

- **No one has tracked skills over years.** The learning studies measured skills within hours of the task. We do not know whether the gaps grow, shrink, or disappear over a career.
- **The strongest evidence is about learning, not forgetting.** It shows AI can stop you from building a new skill. It does not show AI erases skills you already have.
- **Agents may make it worse.** The Anthropic study used a chat sidebar. The researchers expect agentic tools, which need even less of you, to have a stronger effect.
- **The viral brain scans are overhyped.** MIT's "Your Brain on ChatGPT" EEG study had 54 participants, only 18 in its key session, and was released as a preprint before peer review. Claims that brains were "shrinking" are false. It [measured connectivity, not brain size](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3371808872108801).
- **Notice who funded the research.** The main coding study comes from Anthropic, a company that sells AI coding tools. If anything, that makes the negative result more interesting. But the independent PNAS and METR results matter too.

And some skill loss is normal.

Most of us stopped managing memory by hand when garbage collectors arrived. Forgetting the exact argument order of an API you use twice a year is not a crisis.

The real question is:

**Which skills can I afford to lose, and which ones can I not?**

## What AI-first teams are actually doing

While researching this, I noticed something interesting.

The teams getting the most from AI are not the ones generating the most code. They are the ones putting structure around it.

Google's [2025 DORA report](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report) surveyed nearly 5,000 technology professionals. About 90% use AI at work, and more than 80% believe it has made them more productive. AI is now linked to higher delivery throughput.

But it is still linked to **lower delivery stability**.

DORA's summary is that AI is an amplifier. It makes strong teams stronger and makes struggling teams' problems louder.

So what are the stronger teams doing? Five practices keep showing up.

### 1. Writing the spec before the prompt

Spec-driven development has become one of the main answers to "vibe coding." Instead of one big prompt, you write down what the feature should do, then a technical plan, then small tasks, and only then let an agent implement.

```
Spec → Plan → Tasks → Implement → Review
```

GitHub's open-source [Spec Kit](https://visualstudiomagazine.com/articles/2026/05/12/github-spec-kit-takes-off-as-antidote-to-piecemeal-vibe-coding.aspx), released in September 2025, now works with more than 30 AI coding agents. Even its maintainer compares agents to a very capable intern who needs close oversight.

### 2. Keeping agent context in the repository

Teams now store instructions for agents next to the code:

- **AGENTS.md** holds project conventions, build and test commands, and rules any agent should follow. Since August 2025 it has been [adopted by more than 60,000 open-source projects](https://openai.com/index/agentic-ai-foundation/), and it is now governed by the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation.
- **Tool-specific files** such as `.github/copilot-instructions.md` do the same job for a single tool.
- **Agent Skills** are reusable `SKILL.md` folders that teach an agent one procedure, like how your team writes migrations or cuts a release. Anthropic introduced the format in October 2025 and [published it as an open standard in December 2025](https://aibusiness.com/foundation-models/anthropic-launches-skills-open-standard-claude). By April 2026, [more than 30 tools supported it](https://noqta.tn/en/news/skill-md-open-standard-30-ai-coding-tools-adoption-2026), including GitHub Copilot, Codex CLI, and Gemini CLI.

A simple mental model: AGENTS.md is what every agent should always know about this repo. A skill is a playbook the agent loads only when a task needs it.

### 3. Small batches and strong safety nets

DORA's [AI Capabilities Model](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/introducing-doras-inaugural-ai-capabilities-model) lists seven practices that amplify AI's benefits. Two of them are very traditional: **strong version control** and **working in small batches**.

When AI increases the volume of change, small PRs, frequent commits, automated tests, and easy rollback are what keep stability from slipping.

### 4. Connecting AI to real internal context

Two more DORA capabilities are about data: healthy internal data, and AI tools that can actually reach it. This is where retrieval and MCP come in. I wrote about when you actually need each of them in [RAG, MCP, Agents, and Microsoft Foundry](https://sakirsathe.com/writing/rag-mcp-agents-microsoft-foundry-what-do-you-actually-need).

### 5. A clear AI stance and a focus on users

The last three are organizational: a clearly communicated position on which tools are allowed and how to use them, quality internal platforms, and a deep focus on the end user. Without that user focus, DORA found AI adoption can actually hurt team performance.

### Why this matters for skill atrophy

Look at what those practices ask humans to do:

- write clear specifications
- design the plan and break it into small tasks
- curate context and conventions for agents
- review, test, and roll back

**AI-first does not mean human-last.**

The best AI-first practices move developers up the stack, toward specification, design, and review. Those are exactly the skills that passive AI use weakens.

## The skills that matter now

My rule is simple:

**Protect the skills you need to judge AI output. Learn the skills you need to direct it. Let rote syntax go.**

If I cannot tell whether generated code is correct, I am no longer supervising the tool. I am trusting it.

### Skills I would protect

| Skill | Why it matters more with AI | Quick self-test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Debugging | AI code is often almost right, and the bug is yours to find | Fix a failing test using only logs and a debugger |
| Reading code | You now approve far more code than you write | Explain a 50-line AI diff, line by line, before running it |
| Data structures and complexity | AI happily writes O(n²) loops that pass small tests | Spot the slow path in a function and name a faster structure |
| Concurrency and state | Race conditions, deadlocks, and async bugs hide in plausible code | Explain where two requests could interleave or an async call could block |
| Language and runtime basics | Errors, memory, scoping, and types decide whether code works | Write a small utility from a blank file with autocomplete off |
| System design | AI optimizes the snippet, not the architecture | Sketch how a feature fits your system before prompting for it |
| Security basics | Generated code repeats insecure patterns it has seen | Find the injection, auth, or secret-handling flaw in a diff |

### Skills worth learning

| Skill | What it looks like in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Writing specs | Clear acceptance criteria, edge cases, and non-goals an agent can follow |
| Breaking work down | Turning a feature into small tasks that are easy to review and roll back |
| Context engineering | Keeping AGENTS.md, instruction files, and team skills accurate so agents follow your conventions |
| Reviewing at scale | Reading AI diffs for what is missing, not just what is wrong |
| Testing strategy | Tests that catch "almost right" code before it ships |
| Choosing the right tool | Knowing when a problem needs an agent, retrieval, MCP, or plain code |

Safe to let fade: exact syntax for APIs you rarely touch, boilerplate, and config formats you can look up in seconds.

## How I would use AI without losing the skill

The goal is not to use AI less.

The goal is to **keep your brain in the loop**.

The research makes the difference look roughly like this:

```
Delegating:  Prompt → Paste → Tests pass → Ship
Engaged:     Try → Get stuck → Ask why → Understand → Ship
```

Both ship the feature. Only one leaves you better at your job.

### While you work

1. **Try first, then ask.** Give yourself 15 minutes, or the first pass of a new task, before reaching for AI. Getting stuck is where the learning happens.
2. **Ask why, not just what.** Request the code and an explanation together. Then ask a follow-up about the part you understood least.
3. **Predict before you run.** Before accepting AI code, say what it will do and which edge cases could break it. If you can't, you are not ready to merge it.
4. **Debug it yourself first.** Read the stack trace and form a hypothesis before pasting the error into a chat. Use AI to check your theory, not to replace it.
5. **Explain it back.** After a task, describe the solution in your own words: in a commit message, a PR description, or to a teammate. Gaps show up fast.
6. **Use learning modes when you are learning.** Claude Code's Learning and Explanatory modes and ChatGPT's Study Mode are designed to teach rather than hand over answers.

### Outside of task work

- **Do a no-AI rep.** Once a week, solve one small problem with AI and autocomplete turned off. Treat it like a gym session, not a test.
- **Keep one project by hand.** A small side project where you write the code yourself, ideally in a language or domain you want to learn.

## How to keep yourself up to date

The tools change every few months. That makes it tempting to let AI summarize everything for you.

For the things that matter, I would do the opposite.

- **Go to primary sources first.** Release notes, changelogs, official docs, and RFCs. AI training data lags behind new releases, so it may confidently describe last year's version.
- **Try new things in a sandbox repo.** Keep a small throwaway repository for experiments: a new framework version, a new agent, a new MCP server. Write the first example by hand, then try it with AI.
- **Follow a few practitioners, not a firehose.** Pick a handful of engineers and research groups you trust and read them properly, instead of skimming fifty headlines a day.
- **Read research critically.** Check the sample size, who funded it, and what was actually measured. The "brain shrinking" headlines are a good reminder of why.
- **Use AI to test you, not just tell you.** After learning something new, ask AI to quiz you or poke holes in your explanation.
- **Read other people's code.** Review PRs carefully and read well-known open-source projects in your stack.
- **Write and teach.** Blog posts, internal talks, and mentoring force you to understand something well enough to explain it.

A simple rhythm I would start with:

```
Weekly:     30 minutes of release notes for your stack
            1 no-AI practice problem
Monthly:    1 new tool or feature, tried in a sandbox repo
Quarterly:  Time a familiar task with and without AI
            Update AGENTS.md and team skills with what you learned
```

The quarterly timing check matters more than it looks. The METR results show your gut feeling about productivity is a poor measure.

## If you are a junior developer

You have the most to lose, because you are still building the skills experienced developers can coast on for a while.

The Anthropic researchers say this directly: juniors under pressure to move fast may trade real skill growth for speed.

- **Use AI as a tutor while learning** a language, framework, or codebase. Save delegation for things you already understand.
- **Struggle a little on purpose.** Every bug you fix yourself builds the skill that showed the biggest gap in the research.
- **Learn the new skills early.** Practice writing a short spec and reviewing an AI diff line by line. These are quickly becoming core junior skills.
- **Practice without it.** In interviews and during incidents, you will often work without AI.

---

## If you lead a team

If the only thing you measure is speed, you will get delegation.

And the skill loss will stay invisible until an incident.

- **Make learning an explicit goal** for junior developers, not just ticket throughput.
- **Ask for explanations in code review.** Authors should be able to explain AI-written changes, not just confirm the tests pass.
- **Rotate debugging and incident work** so everyone keeps those skills in practice.
- **Invest in the boring foundations.** Small batches, strong version control, and a clear AI policy are among the practices DORA found make AI pay off. Add good tests and an up-to-date AGENTS.md so agents follow your conventions.
- **Leave room for engaged AI use.** In the Anthropic study it cost only a few minutes compared with full delegation.

---

## The question I would ask

Before accepting the next block of AI-generated code, one question is worth asking:

**Could I explain this, and could I fix it if it broke at 2 a.m.?**

If yes, AI made you faster.

If no, AI did the work, and you skipped the learning.

AI is not rotting our brains.

But it will happily do our learning for us if we let it.

The developers who do best in an AI-first world will not be the ones who type the least. They will be the ones who still understand everything they ship.

The tools will keep getting better. Our ability to judge what they produce is the part only we can maintain.
