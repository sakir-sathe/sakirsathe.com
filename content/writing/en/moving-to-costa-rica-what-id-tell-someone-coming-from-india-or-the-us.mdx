Costa Rica was not a grand plan when I moved here in December 2017. I was 24, coming from India, and had completed a Bachelor of Engineering in Information Technology from Mumbai University. My job sent me here to work as part of a nearshore software team. That was the reason for the move; making Costa Rica home came over time.

Now, when somebody finds out I live here, the questions come quickly: How expensive is it? Can I get an IT job? Can I manage without Spanish? And, especially from Indians, what about the food?

I have answered these questions many times and even made a YouTube FAQ years ago. But living somewhere for years changes your answers. You still enjoy the beaches and mountains, but you also know which supermarket is expensive and why something on Amazon costs $40 but feels like a luxury item here.

This is the conversation I would have with a friend who asked:

> “Sakir, I am seriously thinking about moving to Costa Rica. Tell me what I should know before I do it.”

## First: I genuinely like living here

Let me start here: Costa Rica has been very good to me. The country is beautiful, and a few hours' drive can take you to mountains, volcanoes, rainforest, beaches or hot springs. But for me, what makes living here work is the balance.

You can have a serious technology career and still spend a weekend in La Fortuna, Guanacaste or Manuel Antonio without taking a flight. The time zone also works extremely well with North American companies, which matters when you work in technology.

The economy is not built only around tourism. In the first half of 2026, Costa Rica exported more than $6 billion in services excluding travel, and ICT services grew 7% year over year. <a href="https://www.comex.go.cr/sala-de-prensa/comunicados/2026/octubre/cp-3267-exportaciones-de-servicios-superan-los-6000-millones-y-crecen-3-al-primer-semestre-servicios-empresariales-lideran-el-dinamismo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Comex</a> That helps explain the multinational technology, shared-services, engineering and business operations here.

I like living here. That does not mean I would tell you it is cheap or that everything is easy.

## How much does it really cost to live in Costa Rica?

My first question would be: **where do you want to live, and how do you want to live?** Sharing an apartment in Heredia, cooking at home and taking buses is a different budget from renting a furnished condominium in Santa Ana, owning a car and traveling every weekend.

### Housing will decide most of your budget

My first two-bedroom apartment in Santa Ana was around $1,000 a month. Later, another two-bedroom apartment near Avalon/Santa Ana cost me around $1,200. Those are my historical rents; similar apartments in that area now appear to be around $1,400–$1,500.

Several years ago, I also lived in a studio in Pozos for around $500 a month. Santa Ana/Pozos still has many studio-style apartments, with some basic or gated options around $500–$600 depending on the property. A studio and a larger condominium are very different starting points for your budget.

Current Santa Ana listings show two-bedroom apartments around $1,150–$1,600, with newer, furnished or more premium properties going higher. Modern one- and two-bedroom San José listings can reach $1,250–$1,800 depending on location and furnishing. <a href="https://www.encuentra24.com/costa-rica-es/bienes-raices-alquiler-apartamentos/san-jose-provincia-santa-ana?q=f_rooms.2-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">encuentra24.com</a> These are approximate market observations, not a promise that you will find the same apartment at the same price.

### My planning number

For a single professional living independently in the Greater Metropolitan Area, I would personally plan around **$1,500–$2,000+ per month**. For a couple or family, I would start closer to **$2,500+**, then look separately at housing, transportation, healthcare and childcare or school.

These are planning buffers, not official averages. I would rather arrive with some room in the budget than build the whole move around the cheapest apartment found online.

## Where should you live?

For most professionals, I would start with **where you will work**, then choose where to live. An attractive apartment is only part of the decision; the daily journey matters too.

### Santa Ana and the west side

Santa Ana, Lindora and nearby areas have modern condominiums, restaurants, shopping, gyms and office parks. If your office is nearby or you work remotely and like the area, it can be very comfortable. Just don't budget as though you have chosen the cheapest part of Costa Rica.

### Heredia

Heredia makes sense if you work around its large business parks and technology operations. Depending on the exact area, airport access can also be good.

### San José

San José gives you more urban life, restaurants, hospitals, offices and services, but neighborhoods differ enormously. I would not sign a year's lease based only on an attractive online listing.

### Alajuela

Alajuela and surrounding residential areas can offer better value, particularly if you do not need to be in central San José every morning. Airport access is another advantage.

### Beach towns

Living near Tamarindo, Jacó or Nosara can be appealing. But vacation Costa Rica and Tuesday-morning-working-from-home Costa Rica are different things.

Before signing a long lease, stay a few weeks if you can. Check internet at the actual property, electricity reliability, healthcare, supermarkets and transportation. Consider the heat, humidity, rainy-season conditions and how often you need to go to San José.

## Food: cook at home and discover the feria

One recommendation has not changed over the years: **find your local feria.** A *feria del agricultor* is the local farmers' market, and it can be one of the nicest and most economical places to buy fruit, vegetables and other fresh products.

It also becomes part of your routine. You go on Saturday or Sunday morning, buy what you need, perhaps try an unfamiliar fruit, and gradually grocery shopping stops feeling like something you are doing in a foreign country.

Supermarkets are convenient, but imported products get expensive quickly. As a rough restaurant reference, crowd-sourced Numbeo data for San José puts an inexpensive meal around ₡5,000 and a mid-range dinner for two around ₡30,000, with wide variation. <a href="https://www.numbeo.com/cost-of-living/in/San-Jose-Costa-Rica" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Numbeo</a> I would use that as an indication, not an official price survey.

### For Indians: yes, you can find Indian food

You can find Indian restaurants and groceries, but don't expect Mumbai, Delhi or Hyderabad prices. Eating Indian food outside regularly and buying special imported brands can push up the bill.

My approach is simple: **cook most normal meals at home and treat eating out as eating out.** Rice, vegetables, chicken, eggs, beans, fruit and local staples are straightforward.

## Should you bring Indian groceries with you?

I understand wanting to bring familiar food, but don't turn your suitcase into a supermarket. Over the years, packaged dal has sometimes been allowed, while other food items have not. I generally avoid carrying seeds.

This year, Costa Rican airport/agriculture officials confiscated about 4 kg of ghee I was carrying. That surprised and disappointed me because I had carried ghee before. Previous experience clearly wasn't enough to know what would happen this time.

Costa Rica's State Phytosanitary Service warns that seeds, grains, plants, fruits, vegetables, teas, spices and other plant material may be restricted. Requirements vary by product, preparation and country of origin, and items without the appropriate requirements can be retained or destroyed. <a href="https://www.sfe.go.cr/SitePages/Tramites/ComprasenLinea.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SFE</a>

My lesson is simple: commercial packaging does not guarantee permission. Requirements and inspections can vary by product, so check the current official rules before flying.

## Internet and working remotely

Working remotely is realistic in the Greater Metropolitan Area and other well-connected areas. Fiber is increasingly common, but verify availability **at the exact property** before signing a lease.

“Does Santa Ana have fiber?” and “Can this apartment get fiber from this provider?” are different questions. As one reference, Kölbi advertises 100 Mbps home internet at ₡15,000 per month before modem rental, subject to technical availability. <a href="https://www.kolbi.cr/wps/portal/kolbi/hogares/servicios-kolbi-hogar/internet/otros%20planes/internet-hogar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Kölbi</a> Other providers offer different speeds and prices.

As a software engineer, I would also keep mobile data as backup. Video calls, VPNs and remote desktops are not where I want to discover a connectivity problem after moving in.

## What about IT jobs?

Costa Rica has made sense to me professionally because it has a mature multinational-services ecosystem. Software development, cloud, cybersecurity, data, automation, AI, support, finance, engineering and shared-service roles exist here. Technology expansions continued in 2026, including software, AI/ML and cybersecurity positions. <a href="https://www.comex.go.cr/sala-de-prensa/comunicados/2026/mayo/cp-3223-empresa-de-tecnolog%C3%ADa-feuji-se-expande-en-costa-rica-y-abrir%C3%A1-hasta-150-nuevos-puestos-de-trabajo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Comex</a>

People often ask for the average software engineer salary. I find a range more useful:

| Experience level | My rough monthly gross estimate |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | $1,300–$1,800 |
| Mid-level | $2,300–$3,800 |
| Senior | $3,800–$6,000 |
| Strong senior roles at some larger companies | $7,000–$8,000+ |

These are my personal October 2026 estimates, in USD, based on what I have seen in the Costa Rican technology market—not official salary statistics. English, specialization, seniority and the employer all affect the offer.

For software engineers, areas I would focus on include .NET/Java backend engineering, React/Angular, Azure/AWS, DevOps, data engineering, cybersecurity, applied AI and automation/QA.

Then compare **total and net compensation**, not just gross salary. Costa Rica has progressive salary income-tax brackets: for 2026, employment income moves from tax-exempt income through 10%, 15%, 20% and 25% marginal rates. Those rates apply progressively, rather than the highest rate applying to your entire salary. <a href="https://www.hacienda.go.cr/docs/TramosRenta2026.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ministerio de Hacienda</a>

Social-security deductions and benefits also matter. Compare what actually reaches you and the benefits you receive before deciding which offer is better.

## Remote U.S. job versus Costa Rican employment

Local employment and remote work for a foreign company are different arrangements. With local employment, your immigration, payroll, social-security and tax situation follows that arrangement. A foreign remote job does **not automatically give you permission to live or work locally indefinitely**.

Costa Rica's digital-nomad framework is for qualifying remote workers with income from outside the country. The detailed official requirements specify at least **$3,000 per month in foreign-source income for an individual, or $4,000 with dependents**, plus documentation and medical insurance requirements. <a href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/digital-nomads/requirements" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Visit Costa Rica</a>

I would separate three questions: Where is my employer? Where am I legally allowed to live? Where am I legally allowed to work? They are related, but they are not interchangeable.

## Visas and residency: don't plan this from WhatsApp advice

Be careful about immigration advice from friends—including me. Nationality, your reason for staying and your family situation matter.

Current entry guidance requires a valid passport, a visa where applicable, proof of economic means and return or onward travel. The immigration officer determines the authorized stay. <a href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/planning-your-trip/entry-requirements" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Visit Costa Rica</a>

Tourist entry is generally simpler with a U.S. passport. With an Indian passport, I would check directly with Costa Rica's consular office in New Delhi before booking anything significant. <a href="https://www.rree.go.cr/?cat=deCR&amp;cont=525&amp;id=176&amp;pais=IN&amp;sec=misiones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Worship</a> Check your **exact passport, status and travel date**, rather than relying on what worked for someone two years ago.

### DIMEX is an ID, not the immigration category itself

You will hear **DIMEX** constantly. It is the identification document issued to many foreigners granted legal immigration status; the underlying status might be permanent residency, temporary residency, a special category or another authorized stay. <a href="https://www.migracion.go.cr/Documentos%20compartidos/Normativa%20Legal%20Migratoria/Circular%20DIMEX%20que%20se%20encuentran%20actualmente%20vigentes.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Migración Costa Rica</a>

That distinction matters for employment. Immigration law has specific categories and authorizations for different foreign workers. <a href="https://www.migracion.go.cr/Documentos%20compartidos/Leyes/Ley%20General%20de%20Migraci%C3%B3n%20y%20Extranjer%C3%ADa%208764.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Migración Costa Rica</a> Involve your employer's immigration team early if they are relocating you; otherwise, use Migración or a qualified immigration professional.

## Moving with your spouse or family

Start collecting documents **before** the move: marriage and birth certificates, apostilles, translations, police records, insurance and immigration appointments can all become part of the process. The primary applicant's status and a dependent's rights are not necessarily identical, so verify each person's permission if both spouses plan to work.

With children, I would settle the school question before the house question. Private and bilingual schools can be a major expense, and the daily school journey can shape family life.

## Healthcare

The public system is run by the **Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social—CCSS**, usually called *la Caja*. People legally enrolled generally start at their assigned local EBAIS for primary care and can be referred to clinics and hospitals. CCSS confirms that foreigners legally residing in the country can participate under the applicable rules. <a href="https://aissfa.ccss.sa.cr/contacto2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CCSS</a>{/* TODO: replace with exact official source page before next editorial update */}

Private doctors, laboratories, specialists and hospitals are also widely used, particularly for faster scheduling or a specific specialist. Many people use both systems.

Private healthcare prices may feel reasonable compared with the United States and quite different compared with India. My question would be: **what coverage will I have, and what will I use for routine, urgent or specialist care?**

## Banking will probably annoy you at least once

I did not have a major banking horror story. The process was thorough and document-heavy: banks asked for identification, tax-related information and employer documents. The harder part for me was Spanish.

I often used Google Translate and went back and forth with staff. Sometimes communication became frustrating for both sides, but eventually things worked out. It was not a case of staff being unhelpful; explaining documents across a language barrier took effort.

Expect questions about your identity, income, immigration status, employer and sometimes source of funds as part of KYC/anti-money-laundering procedures. Banco Nacional's requirements distinguish residents from non-residents: resident foreigners can use DIMEX or another recognized immigration ID, while non-residents can open certain accounts in person with a valid passport and proof of lawful stay. <a href="https://www.bncr.fi.cr/hubfs/T%C3%A9rminos%20y%20condiciones%20Cuenta%20Expediente%20Simplificado.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">BNCR</a>

Arrive with an international card and financial backup while you get established. For international transfers, compare the full cost: the exchange-rate spread can matter more than the advertised fee.

## Do you need a car?

You can live without a car, particularly if you work remotely or live close to work in the Central Valley. Buses can be economical, and urban ride-hailing helps. Exploring the country becomes easier with a car, but budget for insurance, maintenance, fuel, registration, parking and repairs.

**Do not judge Costa Rican travel by kilometers.**

A destination 20 km away may not feel like a 20 km journey. Check your commute at the hour you would actually travel, not on Sunday afternoon.

### Driving as a visitor

Costa Rica's tourism authority currently states that visitors can use a valid foreign driver's licence for up to three months without needing an international driving permit. <a href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Visit Costa Rica</a>

If you're becoming a resident, check the rules applicable to your immigration status rather than assuming tourist rules continue forever.

## Is Costa Rica safe?

I don't spend my life afraid here, but I would not describe Costa Rica as completely safe either. Learn about the neighborhood where you plan to rent, don't leave laptops or bags visible in a car, and be careful on empty streets or isolated beaches late at night.

The current U.S. State Department advisory is **Level 2: Exercise Increased Caution** because of crime, including petty crime and more serious incidents. <a href="https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/costa-rica.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Travel.state.gov</a> That is useful context alongside understanding the specific places where you spend time.

## How much Spanish do you actually need?

You can survive with English. I would not recommend building your life around surviving.

One of my biggest mistakes was waiting too long to take Spanish seriously. Working in English can make it easy to postpone, but the need does not disappear when you leave the office.

The mechanic, delivery driver, government office or person at the feria may speak Spanish. Even basic Spanish makes those conversations easier, and my bank visits were a good example of why it matters. You don't need to arrive fluent, but I wish I had started earlier.

## The weather is not simply “tropical”

The Central Valley can be mild, Guanacaste hotter and drier, and the Caribbean has a different rainfall pattern. Altitude and local microclimates make a big difference.

Official tourism guidance broadly puts the dry period around December–April and the green/rainy period around May–November, while emphasizing regional differences. <a href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/plan-your-trip/when-to-visit" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Visit Costa Rica</a>

In practice, rainy season does not always mean all-day rain. Plenty of days have a good morning followed by afternoon rain, and I plan around that. The umbrella decision is about later, not just the weather when you leave home.

## If you're Indian, you are not coming to a place with no Indian community

Costa Rica has an established Indian community. The **Costa Rica India Association (CRIA)** has been around since 2010 and organizes Diwali, Holi and other cultural events, as well as supporting newcomers. <a href="https://criaindian.org/events/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CRIA</a>

You will find Indian families, technology professionals, businesses and restaurants. It is a smaller community than in places with hundreds of thousands of Indians, but you are not alone. A circle of Indian, Costa Rican and international friends is one part of living abroad that I value.

## What if you are bringing a dog or cat?

Start planning early. SENASA requires dogs and cats entering Costa Rica to meet veterinary-health requirements, including a clinical examination and official health certificate; vaccination and parasite-treatment requirements also apply depending on the animal. <a href="https://www.senasa.go.cr/informacion/estado-sanitario/alertas-sanitarias/63-cat-informacion/cat-noticias-y-eventos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SENASA Costa Rica</a>{/* TODO: replace with exact official source page before next editorial update */}

Airlines have their own rules, and the return destination has its own import requirements. Plan both journeys.

## The things that are harder than people expect

These are the less exciting parts of the conversation, but they affect ordinary life.

### Bureaucracy

Something that sounds like one document can turn into six, with an apostille, a translation and an appointment along the way. You learn patience.

### Addresses

Directions may describe your location relative to a landmark instead of following the street-number logic someone from the U.S. expects. Eventually it starts making sense. Mostly.

### Imported products

Electronics, specialty food, certain clothes and car parts can cost much more than you expect from India or the United States. Familiar products do not necessarily come with familiar prices.

### Traffic

Mountains, narrow roads, accidents and construction add to the traffic problem. A small country does not always mean a short journey.

### Everything takes a little longer

This was one of my bigger adjustments. My engineering brain likes a process: input, output, done.

Real life is not always an API.

Costa Rica taught me that very effectively.

## What became easier than I expected

Once established, ordinary life becomes more comfortable. You know your supermarket route, bank, doctor and favorite restaurants. You stop converting every colón into dollars or rupees.

Somewhere along the way, “I am living in Costa Rica” becomes “I am going home.” That is difficult to explain until it happens.

## What I wish somebody had told me before I moved

My most personal advice would be to start Spanish earlier and socialize locally more. I waited too long before taking the language seriously, and I think I should have made more effort to socialize locally earlier too. Those are things I would change about my first year.

**Don't optimize your first year for the cheapest possible lifestyle.** Make the transition easier: choose a safe, convenient area you can afford, get reliable internet and give yourself time to understand the country before buying everything. Keep copies of important documents and bring apostilled documents you may need later.

And don't spend every weekend inside San José.

One reason to live in Costa Rica is Costa Rica.

Go see it.

## Mistakes I see newcomers make

A common mistake is expecting Costa Rica to behave like the country you left. Comparing every price with India or every service with the United States can become exhausting.

There are things each country does better. Understanding those differences is more useful than spending your energy being irritated by them. You gradually work out which ones matter to your own life.

## Is Costa Rica right for you?

Costa Rica can be a good fit if you value nature, outdoor travel, a slower pace outside work and a multicultural environment. Relatively easy access to the United States and the remote-work or multinational-services ecosystem can also make professional life work.

I would think more carefully if your priorities are extremely low costs, huge-city nightlife, cheap imported products, highly efficient bureaucracy or living entirely in English. Public transportation and journey times also deserve realistic expectations.

Costa Rica is not India with beaches or California at half price. Eventually you have normal problems here: bills, appointments, work, groceries and rain. You need to like the ordinary version too.

## Would I move to Costa Rica again?

Yes. Knowing what I know now, I would move here again.

I would learn Spanish earlier, organize my documents better and give myself a bigger financial buffer for the first few months. But Costa Rica became much more than a country where I happened to work.

When someone asks whether they should move, I tell them the good and frustrating parts, then suggest spending a few weeks here if they can. Buy groceries, go to the feria, take a bus, drive outside San José and spend a rainy afternoon at home. Talk to people.

You don't learn whether you can **live** somewhere by spending four days at a resort. If the ordinary version still makes you think, **“I could see myself living here,”** that tells you something useful.

## A quick note before you move

Immigration, tax and entry rules change, and your situation will depend on nationality, employment and immigration category. This article reflects my experience plus information researched in October 2026; for an actual move, I would verify the current requirements directly with the relevant Costa Rican authority.
